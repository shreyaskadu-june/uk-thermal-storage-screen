This tool compares the cost of storing heat for later use. Choose one or more technologies and a scale, then the tool gives the cost per kWh delivered, the energy lost while stored, and how sensitive each result is to the inputs. The cost figures come from UK government studies and are shown as ranges, because the sources disagree and some values are assumptions. It is a screening tool, not a design or investment appraisal.

Uncertainties and sensitivities (comparison)

  • The UK cost ranges come from 2016 evidence with no price year. They are restated to 2026 £ with one factor (×1.44), so the real spread is wider than shown.
  • Standing losses, lifetimes and some efficiencies have no UK source and are labelled assumptions. The range column shows the cost if every factor is at its favourable or unfavourable end together.
  • The cost of input energy is the electricity price (16.8p/kWh). Heat pump charging, gas or heat from a network would change the result.

Uncertainties and sensitivities (technologies)

  • Capital cost is the largest driver for all four technologies. For borehole storage the central and high values are assumptions built on a single UK lower bound.
  • Efficiency and standing loss matter most for seasonal storage. Borehole storage uses a seasonal recovery factor instead of a standing loss.
  • Scale matters: the sources give viability thresholds for ground stores and cost benchmarks for large pits that do not transfer to small systems.
03

Method and limits

How the numbers are calculated and what they leave out.

  • Cost per kWh delivered. Annual capital charge (capital recovery factor over the lifetime at the discount rate) plus fixed O&M plus the cost of the energy put in, divided by the energy delivered each year.
  • Energy input. Energy put in per cycle = energy delivered ÷ (conversion efficiency × share retained after storage). Storage time reduces the share retained using the standing loss per day.
  • Seasonal storage. Borehole storage uses its seasonal recovery factor, so no separate standing loss is applied.
  • Price basis. UK sources give no price year. Values are treated as 2015 money and restated with ×1.44 (officialdata.org).
  • Not modelled. Temperature-dependent performance, heat network losses, control strategy, grid services, tariffs and planning. Aquifer storage is excluded (no UK per-kWh cost found). Molten salt, rock bed and thermochemical storage are excluded (no UK or open cost data).

Sources and yearly review

Each input and where it comes from. Re-check every year.

  • DECC/DELTA-EE (2016). Evidence gathering: thermal energy storage technologies, prepared for BEIS. Capital cost ranges (Table 4, Table 10), efficiency (Table 2), O&M reference (§1.3).
  • DESNZ (May 2025). Exploring the take-up and usage of thermal energy storage in heat networks, technical annex RAF062/2324. Operational efficiency for pits and boreholes, lifetime of pit liners, viability threshold for borehole stores (citing Kallesøe et al. 2021).
  • officialdata.org. £1 in 2015 = £1.44 today (restatement factor).
  • Electricity price. DESNZ Energy Trends Q4 2025, manufacturing 16.8p/kWh excl. CCL.
  • Discount rate. 7% investor assumption; 5.18% Ofgem final gas WACC (RIIO-3).
  • Review. Re-check the UK sources when DESNZ or BEIS publish updated thermal storage cost evidence, and at least once a year. Last reviewed 30 October 2026.
